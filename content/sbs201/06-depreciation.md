# Week 6 — Depreciation (IAS 16)

> Sample material written for the Arthur demo. Not an official Strathmore University document.

## Why we depreciate

A non-current asset such as a delivery van is used to earn revenue over several years. Depreciation spreads the cost of the asset over the years that benefit from it, so that each year's expenses are matched with that year's revenue.

Depreciation is **not** a way of saving cash to replace the asset, and it is not an estimate of the asset's market value.

## Key terms

- **Cost:** the purchase price plus costs needed to get the asset ready for use (delivery, installation, legal fees).
- **Residual value:** what the business expects to sell the asset for at the end of its useful life.
- **Useful life:** how long the business expects to use the asset.
- **Depreciable amount:** cost minus residual value. This is the part of the cost that is actually "used up".
- **Carrying amount:** cost minus accumulated depreciation. This is the figure shown in the statement of financial position.

## Method 1: Straight-line

The same charge every year.

**Annual depreciation = (Cost − Residual value) ÷ Useful life**

### Why subtract the residual value?

Students often ask why we cannot just divide the full cost by the useful life. The answer is that the business does not use up the whole cost. If a machine costs KES 240,000 and will be sold for KES 40,000 at the end, the business gets KES 40,000 back. Only KES 200,000 is consumed over the asset's life, so only KES 200,000 should be charged as an expense.

Dividing the full cost would overstate expenses every year and leave the asset at zero, even though it is still worth KES 40,000.

### Worked example

A company buys a machine for **KES 240,000**. Useful life is **4 years**, residual value **KES 40,000**.

Annual depreciation = (240,000 − 40,000) ÷ 4 = **KES 50,000**

| Year | Charge | Accumulated depreciation | Carrying amount |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 50,000 | 50,000 | 190,000 |
| 2 | 50,000 | 100,000 | 140,000 |
| 3 | 50,000 | 150,000 | 90,000 |
| 4 | 50,000 | 200,000 | 40,000 |

At the end of Year 4 the carrying amount equals the residual value.

## Method 2: Reducing balance

A fixed percentage is applied to the **carrying amount at the start of each year**. The charge is highest in the first year and falls every year after.

**Annual depreciation = Rate × Carrying amount at start of year**

Do **not** subtract the residual value first. The rate has already been chosen so that the carrying amount falls to about the residual value by the end of the useful life.

### Worked example

A matatu operator buys a vehicle for **KES 2,000,000** and depreciates it at **25% reducing balance**.

| Year | Opening carrying amount | Charge (25%) | Closing carrying amount |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2,000,000 | 500,000 | 1,500,000 |
| 2 | 1,500,000 | 375,000 | 1,125,000 |
| 3 | 1,125,000 | 281,250 | 843,750 |

## Choosing a method

IAS 16 (paragraph 60) says the method should reflect **the pattern in which the asset's benefits are used up**.

- Use **straight-line** when the asset gives roughly equal benefit each year, such as a building or office furniture.
- Use **reducing balance** when the asset gives more benefit early in its life, or loses efficiency quickly, such as vehicles and computers. A matatu earns most when it is new and reliable; repair costs rise as it ages.

## Double entry

Each year:

- **Dr** Depreciation expense (income statement)
- **Cr** Accumulated depreciation (statement of financial position, deducted from cost)

The asset account itself stays at cost.

## Part-year depreciation

Under the course convention, charge depreciation monthly. A machine costing KES 120,000 with annual straight-line depreciation of KES 24,000, bought on 1 October with a 31 December year end, is charged 3/12 × 24,000 = **KES 6,000** in the first year.

## Common mistakes

1. Subtracting the residual value in the reducing balance method.
2. Applying the reducing balance rate to cost every year instead of the carrying amount.
3. Crediting depreciation directly to the asset account.
4. Forgetting to pro-rate in the year of purchase.
