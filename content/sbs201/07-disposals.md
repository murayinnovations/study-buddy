# Week 7 — Disposal of Non-Current Assets

> Sample material written for the Curriculum AI demo. Not an official Strathmore University document.

When an asset is sold, it must be removed from the books along with its accumulated depreciation. The difference between the sale proceeds and the carrying amount is a **profit or loss on disposal**.

**Profit (loss) on disposal = Proceeds − Carrying amount at the date of sale**

## The disposal account

| Entry | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Remove the asset at cost | Disposal account | Asset (cost) |
| Remove accumulated depreciation | Accumulated depreciation | Disposal account |
| Record the proceeds | Bank | Disposal account |

The balance on the disposal account is the profit (a credit balance) or loss (a debit balance). It is transferred to the income statement.

## Worked example

The machine from Week 6 (cost KES 240,000, straight-line depreciation of KES 50,000 a year) is sold at the end of Year 3 for **KES 110,000**.

- Accumulated depreciation after 3 years: 3 × 50,000 = 150,000
- Carrying amount: 240,000 − 150,000 = 90,000
- Profit on disposal: 110,000 − 90,000 = **KES 20,000**

**Disposal account**

| Debit | KES | Credit | KES |
|---|---|---|---|
| Machine (cost) | 240,000 | Accumulated depreciation | 150,000 |
| Profit on disposal (to income statement) | 20,000 | Bank | 110,000 |
| | **260,000** | | **260,000** |

## Why a profit on disposal is not "real" profit

A profit on disposal usually means depreciation was set too high in earlier years: the asset was worth more than the books said. It is a correction of past estimates, not trading income.

This matters for the statement of cash flows (Week 11): the profit is deducted when working out operating cash flow, and the full KES 110,000 proceeds are shown under investing activities.
