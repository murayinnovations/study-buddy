# Week 11 — Statement of Cash Flows (IAS 7)

> Sample material written for the Arthur demo. Not an official Strathmore University document.

## Why profit and cash differ

A business can be profitable and still run out of cash. Profit is measured on the accrual basis and includes non-cash items such as depreciation. The statement of cash flows shows where cash actually came from and went.

## The three sections

| Section | Includes |
|---|---|
| **Operating activities** | Cash from trading: receipts from customers, payments to suppliers and staff, tax paid, interest paid (course convention) |
| **Investing activities** | Buying and selling non-current assets and investments |
| **Financing activities** | Issuing shares, borrowing and repaying loans, dividends paid (course convention) |

## The indirect method for operating cash flow

Start with **profit before tax** and adjust it back to cash:

1. **Add back non-cash expenses:** depreciation, loss on disposal.
2. **Deduct non-cash income:** profit on disposal (the cash proceeds go under investing).
3. **Adjust for working capital:**
   - Increase in inventory or receivables → **deduct** (cash is tied up)
   - Decrease in inventory or receivables → **add**
   - Increase in payables → **add** (cash not yet paid out)
   - Decrease in payables → **deduct**
4. **Deduct tax paid.**

### Why add back depreciation?

Depreciation was deducted to arrive at profit, but no cash left the business when it was charged. The cash left when the asset was **bought**, and that payment is shown under investing activities in the year of purchase.

## Worked example

Kilele Ltd, year ended 31 December:

- Profit before tax: KES 450,000
- Depreciation: KES 120,000
- Profit on disposal of a machine: KES 20,000 (proceeds KES 110,000)
- Receivables increased by KES 60,000
- Inventory decreased by KES 25,000
- Payables increased by KES 35,000
- Tax paid: KES 90,000
- Purchase of equipment: KES 300,000
- Loan repaid: KES 100,000
- Dividends paid: KES 80,000
- Cash at 1 January: KES 40,000

## Common mistakes

1. Adding an increase in receivables instead of deducting it.
2. Leaving the profit on disposal in operating activities **and** showing the proceeds under investing (counting it twice).
3. Showing the carrying amount of the disposed asset instead of the cash proceeds.
